1 · What BBL Actually Is · 1:10

First misconception to clear up. BBL is not a laser.

BBL stands for BroadBand Light. It's an advanced form of IPL, which is intense pulsed light.

Here's the difference. A laser gives off one single wavelength of light. BBL gives off a whole range at once. Then we use filters to narrow that range depending on what we're going after. That flexibility is the whole point. One device can handle several problems in one pass.

The way it works is simpler than it sounds. Light gets absorbed by things in your skin based on their color.

Brown spots absorb it, because of the melanin. Blood vessels absorb it, because of the hemoglobin. Those targets heat up and get damaged. The normal skin around them absorbs much less, so it's mostly left alone. Then your body clears out the damaged pigment and vessels over the next few days and weeks.

So when I pick a filter for a patient, I'm basically deciding what color I want the light to find.

That's why it's so good at the two things people complain about most, spots and redness. And it's also why it does nothing for problems that don't have a color.

2 · What It Treats, and What It Doesn't · 2:45

BBL is excellent at a specific list and useless at everything else. You should know both halves before you spend money.

What it does well: sun spots and age spots on the face, chest, and backs of the hands. General redness and the fine visible vessels of rosacea. Uneven, dull-looking tone. Freckles, if you want them lightened. You also get a modest improvement in texture over a series, mostly as a bonus.

Patients are always most impressed by the brown spots. That can be dramatic after one or two sessions.

What it does not do: it doesn't tighten loose skin. It doesn't fill deep wrinkles. It doesn't treat acne scars or real texture damage. And it will not do what a resurfacing laser does.

So if your main concern is sagging or lines, BBL is the wrong tool. And anyone selling it to you for that is selling you the wrong thing.

I'm careful about expectations for exactly this reason. A patient who comes in for brown spots is almost always thrilled. A patient hoping to look lifted is going to be disappointed. And that's a conversation we should have had before she paid.

One more thing people bring up. There's a small study out of Stanford suggesting that doing BBL repeatedly over time shifted gene activity in the treated skin toward a younger pattern. Plus some long-term follow-up of patients treated a few times a year.

I find it genuinely interesting. And I'll be straight with you. It's early, small evidence. It's not the same tier as what we have for retinoids or sunscreen. Treat it as a nice bonus, not the reason you book.

So if spots and redness are your thing, this is a strong option. Let's talk about what the appointment is actually like.

3 · The Session and the Recovery · 4:15

The appointment is quick. The recovery surprises people in one specific way.

You come in with clean skin, no makeup. We put protective eyewear on you, and that is non-negotiable, this light will damage your eyes. Then a layer of cool gel, and we move the handpiece across your skin in overlapping pulses.

A full face takes about fifteen to twenty minutes. It feels like a rubber band snap with a flash of heat. Most patients don't need numbing. I'd call it uncomfortable, not painful.

Right after, expect to look flushed and a little swollen for a few hours to a day. Like a mild sunburn.

And here's the part nobody warns people about. It's why I keep photos in my office.

Your brown spots get darker before they leave. Over the next day or two they turn deep brown. Almost like coffee grounds sitting on your skin. Then over about five to ten days they flake off.

So for about a week, you are spottier than when you started. And then you're clearer than you've been in years. Don't pick at them. Let them come off on their own.

I had a patient text me a panicked photo on day two of her first session. She was sure something had gone wrong. Nothing had gone wrong. That's exactly what success looks like on day two. She's on twice-a-year maintenance now.

So plan your calendar. Makeup is usually fine the next day. But don't schedule this a week before an event.

4 · How Many Sessions, and Keeping the Result · 5:45

Let's talk about what to actually plan for. One session is rarely the whole story.

To really clear years of sun damage, most patients need a series. Usually three to five sessions, a few weeks apart, depending on how much we're clearing.

Redness usually needs more sessions than brown spots, because vessels come back. Pigment, once it's cleared, stays cleared unless you make more.

After that, maintenance is usually a couple of sessions a year.

And here's the honest framing. BBL clears the damage that's already there. It does not stop you from making new damage. If you do a series and then spend next summer without sunscreen, you will rebuild the same spots. And I will see you back with the same complaint.

The result only lasts as long as your sun protection does.

This is also why I schedule these in fall and winter. Treating recently tanned skin really raises the risk of a burn and of discoloration. So I want you out of the sun for a few weeks before, and wearing SPF daily after. That's not a scheduling preference. That's a safety call.

Aftercare is simple. Gentle skincare for a few days. Pause retinoids and acids until the flaking is done. No hot yoga or saunas for a couple of days. And strict sun protection.

5 · Who Should Not Get BBL · 7:10

If you remember one thing, make it this. Because this is where people get hurt.

BBL targets pigment. So it can't easily tell the difference between the brown spot you want gone and the melanin in the skin around it.

That means the more pigment your skin has to begin with, the higher the risk that the energy lands where you didn't want it. That can mean a burn. It can mean patches of skin that end up lighter or darker afterward.

That risk goes up a lot in deeper skin tones. And it goes up for anyone who's currently tanned, even a light tan from one weekend outside. A recent tan is a reason to reschedule. Every time.

Melasma is its own warning. Melasma reacts to heat as well as light. So BBL can make it worse instead of better, sometimes dramatically. It might look improved for a few weeks and then come back darker.

If you have melasma, you need a provider who recognizes it and has a plan. And often the right answer is a different treatment entirely.

Also on the not-right-now list: an active infection or cold sore in the area, pregnancy, recent isotretinoin depending on your provider's protocol, medications that make you sun-sensitive, and any spot in the treatment area that hasn't been checked out yet.

So here's your rule. Ask who's running the device and what their training is. Ask if they're doing a test spot. And ask specifically how they're adjusting for your skin tone.

A good provider will answer all three immediately and confidently. If the answers are vague, that's your information.

So the whole thing comes down to this. BBL is excellent for sun spots and redness. Useless for sagging. It takes a series plus maintenance. It gets darker before it gets better. And it belongs in the hands of someone choosing settings for your specific skin.
