1 · What BBL Actually Is · 1:10

Let's clear up the first misconception: BBL is not a laser. BBL stands for BroadBand Light, and it's an advanced form of IPL — intense pulsed light.

Here's the difference that matters. A laser emits one single wavelength of light. BBL emits a broad range of wavelengths at once, and we shape that range with filters depending on what we're targeting. That flexibility is the whole point — it means one device can address several different problems in one pass.

The mechanism is called selective photothermolysis, and the concept is simpler than the name. Light energy is absorbed preferentially by specific targets in your skin based on their color. Melanin — your brown spots — absorbs it. Hemoglobin in your blood vessels — your redness and broken capillaries — absorbs it. Those targets heat up and are damaged, while the surrounding normal skin, which absorbs much less, is largely left alone. Your body then clears the damaged pigment and the treated vessels over the following days and weeks.

So when I'm choosing a filter for a patient, I'm literally deciding what color I want the light to find. That's why this treatment is so effective on the two most common complaints I hear — spots and redness — and why it's ineffective for problems that don't have a color.

Which is the honest place to start on results: what it treats well, and what it doesn't touch.

2 · What It Treats — and What It Doesn't · 2:45

BBL is genuinely excellent at a specific list, and mediocre-to-useless at everything else. Both halves are worth knowing before you spend money.

What it does well: sun spots and age spots, the flat brown lentigines on the face, chest and the backs of the hands. Diffuse background redness and the fine visible vessels of rosacea. Overall unevenness and dullness. Freckling, if you want it reduced. It also gives a modest improvement in tone and texture over a series, mostly as a secondary effect. Patients are consistently most impressed by the brown spots, and that improvement can be dramatic after one or two sessions.

What it does not do: it does not tighten loose skin, it does not fill deep wrinkles, it does not treat acne scarring or significant textural damage, and it will not do what a resurfacing laser does. If your main concern is laxity or lines, BBL is the wrong tool and someone selling it to you for that is selling you the wrong thing.

I'm careful with expectations for this reason. A patient who comes in for brown spots is almost always thrilled. A patient who comes in hoping to look lifted is going to be disappointed, and that was a conversation we should have had before she paid.

There's also a body of research people bring up — a small study out of Stanford suggesting that repeated BBL over time shifted gene expression in treated skin toward a younger pattern, along with long-term observational follow-up of patients treated a few times a year. I find it genuinely interesting, and I also want to be straight with you: it's early, small-scale evidence, not the same tier as what we have for retinoids or sunscreen. Treat it as a promising bonus, not the reason to book.

So if spots and redness are your concern, this is a strong option. Let's talk about what the appointment is actually like, because people build it up in their heads.

3 · What the Session and the Recovery Feel Like · 4:15

The appointment is genuinely quick, and the recovery surprises people in a specific way.

You'll come in with clean skin, no makeup. We put on protective eyewear — non-negotiable, this light will damage your eyes — apply a layer of cool gel, and then move the handpiece across the treatment area in overlapping pulses. The face takes roughly fifteen to twenty minutes. The sensation is the classic description: a rubber band snap with a flash of heat. Most patients tolerate it without numbing, and I'd call it uncomfortable rather than painful. Afterward, expect to look flushed and slightly swollen for a few hours to a day, similar to a mild sunburn.

Here's the part nobody warns people about, and it's the reason I show photos in my office. Your brown spots get darker before they leave. Over the next day or two they turn deep brown, almost like coffee grounds sitting on the surface. Then over roughly five to ten days they flake off. You are, for about a week, more spotty than when you started — and then you're clearer than you've been in years. Do not pick at them, and let them come off on their own.

I had a patient text me a panicked photo on day two of her first session, convinced something had gone wrong. Nothing had gone wrong. That's exactly what success looks like on day two. She's now on twice-yearly maintenance.

So plan the calendar accordingly: makeup is usually fine the next day, but don't schedule this a week before an event. Which brings up how many sessions this actually takes.

4 · How Many Sessions, and How to Keep the Result · 5:45

Here's what to plan for realistically, because a single session is rarely the whole story.

For meaningful correction of accumulated sun damage, most patients need a series — commonly three to five sessions spaced a few weeks apart, depending on how much damage we're clearing. Redness tends to need more sessions than brown spots, because vessels come back and pigment, once cleared, stays cleared unless you make more. After the correction phase, maintenance is typically a couple of sessions a year.

And that's the honest framing I want you to have: BBL clears the damage that's already there. It does not stop you from making new damage. If you have a series and then spend next summer without sunscreen, you will rebuild the same spots, and I will see you back with the same complaint. The result is only as durable as your sun protection.

This is why I schedule these in the fall and winter. Treating recently tanned skin significantly raises the risk of a burn and of dyspigmentation, so I want patients out of the sun for a few weeks before, and committed to daily SPF afterward. That seasonal timing isn't a scheduling preference, it's a safety decision.

Aftercare is simple: gentle skincare for a few days, pause retinoids and acids until the flaking is done, no hot yoga or saunas for a couple of days, and strict sun protection. Which leads directly to the most important section of this video.

5 · Who Should Not Get BBL · 7:10

If you remember one thing, make it this one — because this is where the harm happens.

BBL targets pigment. That means it cannot easily distinguish between the brown spot you want gone and the melanin in the surrounding skin. So the more pigment you have in your baseline skin, the higher the risk of the light delivering energy where you didn't intend it — which can mean burns, and can mean areas of lightened or darkened skin afterward. That risk goes up sharply in deeper skin tones, and it goes up in anyone who is currently tanned, even a light tan from a weekend outside. A recent tan is a reason to reschedule, every time.

Melasma is its own warning. Melasma is heat-sensitive as well as light-sensitive, and BBL can make it worse rather than better, sometimes dramatically. It may look improved for a few weeks and then rebound darker. If you have melasma, you need a provider who recognizes it and has a plan for it — and often the answer is a different treatment entirely.

Also on the not-right-now list: active infection or cold sore in the area, pregnancy, recent isotretinoin depending on your provider's protocol, photosensitizing medications, and any undiagnosed changing lesion in the treatment zone, which needs to be evaluated before we put light on it.

So the rule is this: ask who is operating the device and what their training is, ask whether they're doing a test spot, and ask specifically how they're accounting for your skin tone. A good provider will have immediate, confident answers. If the answers are vague, that's your information.

So the whole thing comes down to this: BBL is excellent for sun spots and redness, useless for laxity, takes a series with maintenance, gets darker before it gets better, and belongs in the hands of someone who chooses settings for your specific skin — not a device someone bought and pointed at everyone.
