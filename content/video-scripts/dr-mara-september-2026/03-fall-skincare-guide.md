1 · What Actually Changes in the Fall · 1:10

Let's be precise about this, because "the air gets dry" doesn't tell you what to do about it.

Your outer layer of skin holds water, and how well it holds it depends on the moisture in the air around it. Summer air holds a lot of water, so your skin is being passively hydrated all day, even when the humidity feels miserable. When temperatures drop, the air physically can't hold as much moisture, so water leaves your skin faster. Then we make it worse indoors — heated air can drop the relative humidity in your home down into the teens, which is desert-level dry, and you're in it for the next seven months.

So your barrier is working harder to hold water while the environment pulls it out faster. That's why the complaints in my clinic shift so predictably in October: tightness after cleansing, flaking around the nose and brows, fine lines looking more pronounced, lips splitting, hands cracking, and eczema and dandruff flaring on schedule.

I can almost set a calendar by it. The same patients come in the second week of October every year telling me their skin "suddenly" changed. It didn't change suddenly — the dew point did, and their routine didn't move with it.

So the goal for fall isn't a new routine. It's the same routine, made slightly richer and slightly gentler. That starts with the two products you use every day.

2 · The Two Swaps That Do the Most Work · 2:40

If you only make two changes this season, make these — your cleanser and your moisturizer.

Your summer cleanser was probably a gel or a foaming formula, and that was the right call when you were sweating through sunscreen every day. In fall, that same cleanser strips lipids you can no longer spare. Move to a cream, milk, or gel-cream cleanser that doesn't foam much. And here's the test: if your face feels squeaky and tight afterward, that's not clean, that's stripped. For most people, cleanser at night and water only in the morning is enough once the weather turns.

Then your moisturizer needs to get richer, and you want three things working together. Humectants like glycerin and hyaluronic acid pull water in. Ceramides and fatty acids repair the barrier itself. And an occlusive layer on top stops that water from evaporating. This is why a hyaluronic acid serum alone can backfire in dry air — a humectant with nothing sealing it can pull water up from deeper in the skin and let it evaporate away. It needs a cream over it.

The habit that matters as much as the product: apply within about three minutes of washing, onto slightly damp skin. My patients who make that one timing change consistently report a bigger difference than the ones who upgraded to a more expensive cream.

Once the barrier is supported, you can do the thing fall is actually for — treating what the summer did.

3 · Restarting Your Retinoid the Right Way · 4:00

If you backed off your retinoid over the summer, and most people quietly do, this is the month to restart. And how you restart determines whether you're still using it in December.

The reason fall is right: lower UV, less sweating, no beach weekends undoing your progress — and retinoids need months of consistency to deliver, so starting now means results by spring. Retinoids increase cell turnover, build collagen, fade pigment, and prevent clogged pores. They are still the most evidence-backed anti-aging ingredient we have.

Here's how I have patients restart, and it's slower than you think. Assume you've lost your tolerance, even if you were on it for years. Two nights a week, a pea-sized amount for the entire face, applied to completely dry skin — damp skin drives it in faster and irritates. Build up over about six weeks. And if you're sensitive, use the sandwich: moisturizer, then retinoid, then moisturizer on top. That reduces irritation substantially without meaningfully reducing the benefit.

The patients who fail with retinoids almost never fail because the product was wrong. They fail because they went nightly in week one, got red and peeling by week three, and quit. Slow is the whole strategy.

And while you're restarting the retinoid, this is also the window for the pigment that got worse over the summer.

4 · This Is Pigment and Procedure Season · 5:30

Here's the part I care about most, and it's the reason dermatology clinics get busy in the fall.

Every treatment we have for pigmentation and resurfacing shares one weakness: sun undoes it. Trying to fade melasma or sun spots in July, while you're outside every weekend, is filling a bucket with a hole in it. From now through early spring, the UV index is lower and your exposure is lower, so the treatments finally get to work.

Topically, that means a retinoid, azelaic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C in the morning, topical tranexamic acid for melasma, and hydroquinone in supervised cycles when it's appropriate. And in the office, this is when we schedule the things we can't safely do in summer: chemical peels, resurfacing lasers, and light-based treatments like BBL for sun spots and redness. Those carry real risk on recently tanned skin, and they all require strict sun protection afterward — which is exactly why we do them now.

My melasma patients hear the same thing from me every September. We spent the summer defending; now we treat. And the ones who use this window consistently look noticeably different by March, while the ones who wait until spring start over every year.

So book the treatment now if you've been putting it off — and then protect the result, which brings me to the mistake almost everyone makes.

5 · The Thing Everybody Drops in October · 7:00

If you make one commitment from this video, make it this one. Do not stop wearing sunscreen.

Every fall, sunscreen is the first product to go. And here's why that's the wrong read: UVB, the burning ray, does drop off substantially. But UVA — the ray that drives collagen breakdown, wrinkles, and pigment — stays much more constant year-round. It penetrates cloud cover and it passes through window glass, so your commute and the desk you sit at next to a window are both dosing you every day.

There's also a timing issue specific to this season. Everything I just recommended — a retinoid restart, pigment treatment, a peel or a laser — makes your skin more photosensitive and more prone to rebound pigmentation. Skipping sunscreen while doing those things doesn't just stall your progress, it can actively reverse it.

I've had patients do a beautiful course of treatment in the fall, get busy, drop their SPF through the winter, and come back in April having lost most of the improvement. Nothing about the treatment failed. The maintenance did.

So keep the sunscreen daily, all year. And if you're treating pigmentation, keep it tinted — the iron oxides that create the tint are what block visible light, which drives melasma even when the UV index is low.

So the whole thing comes down to this: soften your cleanser, richen your moisturizer, restart your retinoid slowly, use the treatment window while it's open, and never drop your SPF. Five changes, and most of them use products you already own.
