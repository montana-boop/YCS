1 · What Actually Changes in the Fall · 1:10

Let's be specific about this. Because "the air gets dry" doesn't tell you what to do about it.

Your skin holds onto water. How well it holds it depends on the moisture in the air around it.

Summer air holds a lot of water. So even when the humidity feels miserable, your skin is getting hydrated all day for free. Then the temperature drops. The air can't hold as much moisture anymore. So water leaves your skin faster.

And then we make it worse indoors. Heated air can drop the humidity in your house down into the teens. That's desert-level dry. And you're living in it for the next seven months.

So your skin is working harder to hold water, while the air pulls it out faster. That's why my clinic gets the same complaints every October. Tightness after washing. Flaking around the nose and brows. Fine lines looking deeper. Split lips. Cracked hands. Eczema and dandruff flaring right on schedule.

I can almost set a calendar by it. The same patients come in the second week of October and tell me their skin suddenly changed. It didn't change suddenly. The weather did, and their routine didn't move with it.

So the goal for fall is not a new routine. It's the same routine, a little richer and a little gentler. Starting with the two products you use every day.

2 · The Two Swaps That Do the Most · 2:40

If you only change two things this season, change these. Your cleanser and your moisturizer.

Your summer cleanser was probably a gel or a foam. That was the right call when you were sweating through sunscreen every day. In fall, that same cleanser strips you.

Switch to a cream or gel-cream cleanser that doesn't foam much. And here's your test. If your face feels squeaky and tight after, that's not clean. That's stripped.

For most people, cleanser at night and just water in the morning is plenty once the weather turns.

Then your moisturizer needs to get richer. You want three things working together. Humectants like glycerin and hyaluronic acid, which pull water in. Ceramides, which repair the barrier. And something on top to seal it so the water can't evaporate.

This is why a hyaluronic acid serum on its own can backfire in dry air. It pulls water up from deeper in your skin, and then that water just evaporates. It needs a cream over it.

And here's the habit that matters as much as the product. Put your moisturizer on within about three minutes of washing, while your skin is still slightly damp. My patients who change that one thing tell me it made more difference than buying a more expensive cream.

Once your barrier is supported, you can do the thing fall is actually for. Treating what the summer did.

3 · Restarting Your Retinoid · 4:00

If you backed off your retinoid over the summer, and most people quietly do, this is the month to start again. And how you restart decides whether you're still using it in December.

Why fall is right: lower UV, less sweating, and no beach weekends undoing your work. Retinoids need months of consistency. So starting now means real results by spring.

Retinoids speed up cell turnover, build collagen, fade pigment, and keep pores clear. They're still the most proven anti-aging ingredient we have.

Here's how I have patients restart. And it's slower than you think.

Assume you lost your tolerance, even if you used it for years. Two nights a week. A pea-size amount for your whole face. On completely dry skin, because damp skin drives it in faster and irritates. Then build up over about six weeks.

And if you're sensitive, do the sandwich. Moisturizer, then retinoid, then moisturizer on top. That cuts the irritation way down without really cutting the benefit.

The patients who fail with retinoids almost never fail because of the product. They fail because they went every night in week one, got red and peeling by week three, and quit. Slow is the whole strategy.

And while you're restarting that, this is also the window for the pigment that got worse over the summer.

4 · This Is Pigment and Procedure Season · 5:30

Here's the part I care about most. And it's why dermatology offices get busy in the fall.

Every treatment we have for pigment has the same weakness. Sun undoes it.

So trying to fade melasma in July, while you're outside every weekend, is like filling a bucket with a hole in it. From now through early spring, the UV is lower and you're outside less. That's when the treatments finally get to work.

At home that means a retinoid, azelaic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C in the morning, and tranexamic acid for melasma. Hydroquinone too, in cycles, when it's the right fit.

And in the office, this is when we schedule the things we can't safely do in summer. Chemical peels. Resurfacing lasers. Light treatments like BBL for sun spots and redness.

Those carry real risk on recently tanned skin. And they all need strict sun protection afterward. That's exactly why we do them now.

My melasma patients hear the same thing from me every September. We spent the summer defending. Now we treat. The ones who use this window every year look noticeably better by March. The ones who wait until spring start over every single year.

So book it now if you've been putting it off. And then protect the result, which brings me to the mistake almost everyone makes.

5 · The Thing Everybody Drops in October · 7:00

If you make one commitment from this video, make it this. Do not stop wearing sunscreen.

Every fall, sunscreen is the first thing to go. Here's why that's wrong.

UVB, the ray that burns you, really does drop off in the fall. But UVA doesn't. UVA is the one that breaks down collagen and drives wrinkles and pigment, and it stays fairly steady all year.

It also goes through clouds. And it goes through window glass. So your commute and your desk by the window are both dosing you every day.

There's also a timing problem this season. Everything I just recommended makes your skin more sun-sensitive. The retinoid. The pigment treatment. A peel or a laser. So skipping sunscreen doesn't just stall your progress. It can undo it.

I've had patients do a beautiful course of treatment in the fall, get busy, drop the SPF over the winter, and come back in April having lost most of it. The treatment didn't fail. The maintenance did.

So keep it daily, all year. And if you're treating dark spots, keep it tinted. The iron oxides in a tinted sunscreen are what block visible light, and visible light drives melasma even when the UV is low.

So the whole thing comes down to this. Soften your cleanser. Richen your moisturizer. Restart your retinoid slowly. Use the treatment window while it's open. And never drop your SPF. Five changes, and most of them use what you already own.
