**body · 1:00**

**1. Rosacea**

Rosacea is the most common chronic cause of facial redness in adults, so it deserves the top spot, and I'm going to keep it short because I have a whole separate video on it.

The pattern: redness across the center of the face — cheeks, nose, chin, center of the forehead — that persists between flares. Flushing triggered by heat, exercise, alcohol, spicy food, stress, or temperature swings. Visible fine branching vessels. Often red bumps and pus bumps, but never blackheads. Skin that stings when you put things on it. Usually starts after thirty. And frequently dry, gritty, irritated eyes, which nobody connects to their face.

The fix is a gentle routine, daily sunscreen, prescription topicals like azelaic acid or ivermectin, low-dose doxycycline for tougher cases, and laser or IPL for the vessels that creams will never touch.

But here's the thing that got me to make this video. Rosacea has become the default answer for any red face, and it's wrong a lot. So let's talk about what else it is.

**2. Seborrheic Dermatitis**

This is the one that gets misdiagnosed as rosacea more than anything else, and once you know the pattern you'll never miss it again.

Seborrheic dermatitis is an inflammatory reaction to Malassezia, a yeast that lives on everyone's skin, in the areas where you make the most oil. So look at the specific real estate: the creases running from your nose down to the corners of your mouth, the eyebrows and the skin between them, the sides of the nose, sometimes the ears and the hairline. If your redness lives in the creases rather than out on the apples of your cheeks, think seb derm.

The tell is the scale. Seb derm redness comes with a fine, greasy, sometimes yellowish flaking. Rosacea doesn't flake like that. And here's the giveaway question I ask every time: do you have dandruff? Because it's the same condition on your scalp. If you've got flaky brows and an itchy flaky scalp, that's not a coincidence, that's your diagnosis.

Treatment is genuinely satisfying because it works fast. Antifungal shampoo — ketoconazole or zinc pyrithione — used on the face as a short-contact wash, two or three minutes then rinse, a few times a week. Prescription antifungal creams. A short course of a mild anti-inflammatory when it's angry. It flares in cold dry weather and with stress, so expect to manage it, not cure it. But it responds.

**3. Contact Dermatitis — Something Is Touching Your Face**

Now we're into the category where something external is causing it, and the two big ones look similar but happen differently.

Irritant contact dermatitis is chemical damage — something is too harsh and it's physically injuring the skin. Allergic contact dermatitis is your immune system reacting to a specific ingredient, and it's typically itchy, sometimes bumpy or weepy, and it can show up a day or two after exposure, which is why people never connect it to the cause.

The usual suspects: fragrance, which is the single most common cosmetic allergen there is and hides behind the words "parfum," "fragrance," and yes, "essential oils." Preservatives, particularly the methylisothiazolinone family. Botanical extracts. Hair dye and, weirdly often, shampoo and conditioner, which is why some people get a rash on the sides of the face, the neck, and the back of the ears in a rinse-off pattern.

The pattern-recognition trick: contact dermatitis follows exposure geometry. If the redness stops exactly where a product stops, if one side of your face is worse because that's the side you rest on your hand, if the shape has an edge to it — that's contact, not a chronic internal condition. And if it's around the eyelids, look at everything your hands touch, because eyelid skin is the thinnest on your body and it reacts first.

If it keeps coming back and you can't find it, patch testing is a real test we do that identifies the specific allergen. It's not the same as an allergy scratch test, and it's the answer for people who've been going in circles for years.

**4. The Redness You Caused Yourself**

Here it is. The most common cause on this list, and the one nobody wants to hear.

Your barrier is damaged, and it's damaged because of your routine. Retinol four nights a week, a glycolic acid toner, a vitamin C serum, a clay mask on Sundays, a cleansing brush, and a foaming cleanser that squeaks. Every one of those things can be fine in isolation. Stacked together, they strip the lipids out of your outer layer faster than you can rebuild them.

When that barrier is compromised, water evaporates out of your skin faster, your nerve endings are more exposed, and everything you apply stings. So the skin stays inflamed, which reads as red. And here's the cruel part: people interpret that redness and roughness as "my skin looks bad, I need to treat it more," and they add another active. I have watched patients do this for a year.

The signs: your skin stings with products that never used to bother you. It feels tight after washing. It's simultaneously flaky and shiny. It's gotten more reactive over time, not less. And there's no clear pattern to where the redness sits — it's just diffusely irritated.

The fix is free and it's just stopping. I'll walk you through exactly how at the end of the video. Barrier damage is fully reversible, it usually takes two to four weeks, and the hardest part is psychological, because doing nothing feels like giving up.

**5. Perioral Dermatitis**

This one is specific and it traps people for months, so I want you to know it.

Small red bumps, sometimes with tiny pustules, clustered around the mouth, sometimes around the nose and eyes. The signature detail: it spares a thin clear rim of skin right at the border of your lips. That little margin of normal skin is the giveaway.

The cause is often a topical steroid. Somebody has a red patch, they get a steroid cream, it looks better for a week, they keep using it, and then this shows up. And here's the trap — when they stop the steroid it gets dramatically worse for a few days, so they go right back on it. That rebound is exactly what perioral dermatitis does. Heavy occlusive products, inhaled steroids for asthma, and sometimes fluoridated toothpaste play into it too.

Treatment is stopping every steroid on your face and knowing that it will flare before it settles, plus a topical or oral course from your dermatologist. If you've got bumps around your mouth and a tube of hydrocortisone in your bathroom, that's the whole story right there.

**6. Flushing — When It's Coming From the Inside**

Flushing is different from fixed redness. Flushing is episodic: it comes, your face is hot, and then it goes.

The common drivers are alcohol — and there's a genetic version where the enzyme that breaks down alcohol's byproduct works poorly, which causes dramatic flushing after one drink and is much more common in people of East Asian descent. Niacin supplements. Spicy food. Exercise and heat, which are normal. Hot flashes in perimenopause and menopause, which are wildly underdiscussed as a skin complaint. And a handful of medications, particularly some blood pressure drugs.

Most flushing is benign. But if you're having dramatic flushing episodes with other symptoms — diarrhea, wheezing, palpitations, unexplained weight loss — that's a pattern that deserves a workup, because rare conditions can cause it and they're diagnosable.

**7. Sun Damage and Permanent Vessels**

Some redness isn't inflammation at all. It's structure.

Decades of UV exposure break down the collagen that supports the small blood vessels in your skin. Without that scaffolding, the vessels dilate permanently and you can see them. That's telangiectasia, and it's why redness often sits on the cheeks, the sides of the nose, and the chest in people who spent their twenties outside.

There's a classic version on the neck and upper chest — reddish-brown mottled discoloration that characteristically spares the shaded patch right under the chin. That shadow is a photograph of a lifetime of sun, and I point it out to patients constantly.

No cream fixes a dilated vessel. Vascular laser and IPL do, and they do it well. Sunscreen from here forward stops you from making more.

**8. The One That Means See a Doctor**

Last one, and this is why I put this list together.

Some facial redness is a sign of an autoimmune condition. The classic pattern is a butterfly-shaped redness across both cheeks and the bridge of the nose that characteristically spares the creases beside the nose — the exact opposite of seborrheic dermatitis, which lives in those creases. It's often photosensitive: it flares after sun exposure and can take weeks to fade.

What makes me look harder is when the skin finding comes with the rest of the picture: joint pain and morning stiffness, fatigue that isn't normal tiredness, mouth ulcers, hair shedding, fevers, or Raynaud's — fingers going white and then blue in the cold. Facial redness plus those symptoms is a rheumatology conversation and some bloodwork, not a skincare conversation.

Dermatomyositis is another one, with a violet discoloration of the eyelids and a rash over the knuckles.

I'm not telling you this to scare you. The overwhelming majority of red faces on this list are the first seven causes. I'm telling you because the skin is often the first place these announce themselves, and people spend a year buying serums for something that needed a blood test.

**The Two-Week Reset**

Here's what I actually do when a patient walks in and we're not sure which of these it is. We take the noise out.

For two weeks: a gentle non-foaming cleanser, once at night, lukewarm water, hands only, no washcloth, no brush, no scrub. A simple ceramide moisturizer morning and night. A mineral sunscreen every morning. That's it. That's the entire routine. No retinol, no acids, no vitamin C, no masks, no actives, nothing with fragrance.

Take a photo in the same light on day one, day seven, and day fourteen — not in the mirror, because your brain is a terrible instrument for tracking gradual change.

If you're dramatically better in two weeks, congratulations, your problem was your routine, and now you can reintroduce actives one at a time, two weeks apart, and find out which one your skin actually objects to. If you're only somewhat better, you've got a real underlying condition and now we're treating it on calm skin instead of angry skin, which works far better anyway. And if you're no better at all, you've just given your dermatologist very useful information.
