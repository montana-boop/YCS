**body · 1:00**

**Check 1: Where Is It?**

Location is the first thing I look at when I walk into a room, and it's the most useful thing you can check yourself.

Eczema lives in the bends. The inner crease of your elbows, behind your knees, the front of the neck, the wrists, the eyelids, the hands. Warm, moist, flexing areas. In infants it's classically on the cheeks and the outer arms and legs, and it migrates into those creases as kids get older.

Psoriasis does the opposite. Psoriasis loves the outside of joints — the points of your elbows and the fronts of your knees. Plus the scalp, especially along the hairline and behind the ears. The lower back and the crease between the buttocks. The belly button. And the palms and soles in some people.

So the fastest version of this: flip your arm over. Rash in the crease of the elbow points to eczema. Rash on the point of the elbow points to psoriasis. That single move gets you a long way.

**Check 2: What Do the Edges Look Like?**

Get close and look at where the rash stops.

Psoriasis is sharply demarcated. There's a defined edge, like somebody drew it. You can run your finger to the border and there's rash, then there's normal skin, with almost no transition zone. The plaques are thick, raised, and topped with a silvery-white scale that builds up in layers. If you scrape that scale off you can get pinpoint bleeding underneath — that's a real physical sign we use, and please do not go test it on yourself.

Eczema is the opposite. It fades out. Ill-defined borders that blend into normal skin. The scale is finer and drier, not silvery and stacked. When it's acutely flaring it can be weepy, oozing, crusted. When it's chronic and someone's been scratching it for years, the skin gets thick and leathery with exaggerated skin lines — we call that lichenification, and it's basically a scratch record.

Thick, silvery, sharp edges: psoriasis. Fine, dry, blurry edges, sometimes weeping: eczema.

**Check 3: Does It Itch, or Does It Hurt?**

This one's subjective, but it's more useful than you'd think.

Eczema is defined by itch. There's a saying in dermatology that atopic dermatitis is "the itch that rashes" — meaning the itching often comes first and the visible rash is partly a consequence of scratching. It's a deep, maddening itch that wakes people up at night. When I ask an eczema patient about the itch, I get an immediate emotional response, because it runs their life.

Psoriasis itches too — the old teaching that it doesn't is wrong and it minimized a lot of patients. But the quality is different. Psoriasis patients more often describe burning, stinging, soreness, and tightness, especially when a thick plaque cracks and splits. Plaques on the hands and feet can genuinely hurt to use.

**Check 4: Look at Your Fingernails**

Here it is. This is the fastest tiebreaker in this entire video, and almost nobody thinks to look.

Psoriasis affects the nail unit in a large share of patients. You're looking for tiny pinpoint dents in the nail plate, like somebody tapped it with a pin — that's pitting. Yellow-brown discoloration under the nail that looks like a drop of oil under the plate. The nail lifting off its bed starting at the free edge. Crumbly debris built up underneath. Ridges and thickening.

Eczema can change the nails a bit too, mostly ridging and roughness from inflammation at the cuticle, but you don't get the classic pitting and oil-drop pattern.

So if you've got an ambiguous rash and you also have pitted nails, you've likely got your answer. I've made this diagnosis across a table more than once because someone reached for a pen.

**Check 5: What Else Is Going On With You?**

Both of these travel with company, and the company is different.

Eczema is part of what we call the atopic march. Ask about asthma, seasonal allergies and hay fever, food allergies, and a family history of the same. It usually starts in childhood — a large majority of atopic dermatitis begins before age five. There's often a genetic component in a protein called filaggrin that helps build the skin barrier, which is why these patients have chronically dry skin everywhere, not just where the rash is.

Psoriasis travels with joints. Up to a third of psoriasis patients develop psoriatic arthritis, and this is the part I really need you to hear: joint damage from psoriatic arthritis can be permanent, and it's much easier to prevent than to repair. So if you have psoriasis and you've got morning stiffness lasting more than half an hour, swollen fingers or toes that look like sausages, heel pain, or low back stiffness that improves with movement — tell somebody. Do not write it off as getting older.

Psoriasis also runs with metabolic syndrome, higher cardiovascular risk, fatty liver, and depression. It is a systemic inflammatory disease that happens to be visible on the skin. Onset is often in the twenties, with a second wave later in life. And it Koebnerizes — meaning it shows up in places you've injured, like a scratch, a sunburn, a tattoo, or a surgical scar. Guttate psoriasis, a shower of small drop-shaped spots across the trunk, classically follows a strep throat infection in a young person.

**What's Actually Happening Underneath**

Quick science, because it explains why the treatments diverge so completely.

Eczema is fundamentally a barrier problem plus an immune response skewed in one direction — the branch of immunity your body uses for allergy, driven by signals called IL-4 and IL-13. The barrier is leaky, irritants and allergens get in, the immune system reacts, you itch, you scratch, the barrier gets leakier. That's the cycle. Which is exactly why restoring the barrier with moisturizer isn't a comfort measure in eczema, it's actual treatment.

Psoriasis is a different immune pathway entirely — driven by IL-17 and IL-23 — that tells your skin cells to divide far too fast. Normal skin turns over in about a month. Psoriatic skin does it in a matter of days, so the cells pile up before they've matured. That pile is the silvery plaque. And you cannot moisturize a signaling error. Moisturizer makes the plaque more comfortable and softer, and that's worth doing, but it does not touch the driver.

That's the core difference in one line: eczema is a broken wall, psoriasis is a stuck accelerator.

**What Actually Treats Eczema**

Foundation first, and it is not optional. Fragrance-free gentle cleanser, short lukewarm showers, and a thick ceramide-containing moisturizer applied to damp skin within a few minutes of getting out, over your whole body, every single day, including when you're clear. Ointments and creams, not thin lotions in a pump.

For flares, topical steroids used correctly — appropriate strength for the body site, for a defined stretch, not a random dab of hydrocortisone forever. Steroid phobia keeps people undertreated and miserable; steroid misuse thins skin. The answer is instruction, not avoidance. For the face, eyelids and skin folds we use non-steroid options like calcineurin inhibitors so we're not putting steroids on delicate skin long-term.

Diluted bleach baths help patients who flare with infection, because eczematous skin gets colonized with staph. And when eczema is moderate to severe, the last several years have genuinely changed the game — targeted biologics that block those specific immune signals, and oral medications in the same family. Patients who spent twenty years in misery are clear now. If you've been told to just keep moisturizing and you're still covered, you are behind on what's available.

**What Actually Treats Psoriasis**

Limited psoriasis gets topicals: a potent steroid paired with a vitamin D analog is the classic workhorse, and there are newer non-steroid creams that work well without the long-term steroid concerns. Salicylic acid or urea helps lift thick scale so the medication can actually get in — putting steroid on top of a thick plaque is like painting over a rug.

Scalp psoriasis needs its own approach: medicated shampoos, solutions and foams rather than creams, and enough contact time to matter.

For anything widespread, phototherapy — narrowband UVB in a real medical unit, not a tanning bed, and I'm going to be very clear that a tanning bed is not treatment, it's carcinogen exposure with a side of temporary improvement. And then systemic therapy: older pills like methotrexate, and modern biologics targeting IL-17 and IL-23 that can get a large share of patients to nearly clear skin.

I have patients who spent a decade thinking psoriasis meant a life of thick plaques and long sleeves in July, who are now completely clear on an injection every couple of months. That's not marketing, that's what the last ten years of immunology did.

**The Mimics**

Before you diagnose yourself, three things fool everybody.

Fungal infection. A ring-shaped scaly patch with a raised advancing edge and clearing in the middle can look like either one, and if you put a steroid on it, it spreads and gets weirder. A scraping under the microscope settles it in five minutes.

Seborrheic dermatitis on the scalp gets called psoriasis constantly. Seb derm scale is greasier and yellower, sits in oily areas, and has fuzzier borders.

And nummular eczema — coin-shaped, sharply round, scaly patches, usually on the legs, that look enough like psoriasis to fool a trained eye.

If it's not clearly improving in a few weeks of reasonable treatment, that's not a reason to buy something stronger at the drugstore. That's a reason to get looked at, and if it's still ambiguous, a small skin biopsy answers it definitively.
