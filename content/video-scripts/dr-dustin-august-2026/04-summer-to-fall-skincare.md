**body · 1:00**

**What Actually Changes**

Let's be precise about this, because "the air gets dry" is doing a lot of unexamined work in most skincare advice.

Your skin's outer layer holds onto water, and how much it holds depends heavily on the moisture in the air around it. In the summer, the air is holding a lot of water — the dew point is high, and even if it feels sticky and miserable, your skin is being passively hydrated all day. Then fall comes, temperatures drop, the air physically cannot hold as much water, and the amount of water leaving your skin into that drier air goes up.

Then we make it worse indoors. You turn the furnace on and heated indoor air gets extremely dry, often down into the teens or twenties in relative humidity — which is desert territory. And you're breathing that for the next seven months.

So your barrier is working harder to hold water while the environment is pulling it out faster. The result is the stuff you already recognize: tightness after washing, flaking around the nose and eyebrows, that fine crepey look on the cheeks, lips that split, and hands that crack at the knuckles. Eczema flares. Seborrheic dermatitis flares — that's why dandruff always gets worse in October. Psoriasis flares as the daylight drops.

None of that requires new products with the word "autumn" on them. It requires a slightly heavier, slightly gentler version of what you're already doing. So let's do the five changes.

**Change 1: Your Cleanser**

This is the easiest win and the one most people skip.

In summer you're sweating, you're wearing more sunscreen, you're probably using a gel or foaming cleanser, and that's appropriate. In fall, that same cleanser is stripping you. The foaming agents that cut through sweat and sebum in August are removing lipids you can no longer afford to lose.

Move to a cream, milk, or gel-cream cleanser. It shouldn't foam much and your face should not feel squeaky after. Squeaky means you removed too much. That tight feeling isn't clean, it's damage.

And drop to washing your face with cleanser once a day — at night, when you're removing sunscreen and the day. In the morning, water alone is enough for most people. If you have oily or acne-prone skin, keep both washes, just soften the product.

Same principle in the shower: shorter, cooler, and get out. Hot water feels incredible on a cold morning and it's the fastest way to strip your entire body. I'm not going to pretend I've never taken a long hot shower in January. But know the trade you're making.

**Change 2: Your Moisturizer**

Second easy one. Your summer lotion is not going to cut it in November.

You want three categories of ingredient working together, and most good creams have all three. Humectants like glycerin and hyaluronic acid pull water in. Emollients like ceramides and fatty acids fill the gaps between skin cells and repair the barrier. Occlusives like petrolatum, dimethicone, and shea sit on top and stop the water from leaving.

Here's the mistake I see constantly: people buy a hyaluronic acid serum, put it on, and stop there. In dry air, a humectant with nothing sealing it can actually pull water out of the deeper layers of your skin and let it evaporate. So a humectant serum needs a cream on top of it. Always, but especially now.

Timing matters more than people think. Apply moisturizer to slightly damp skin within about three minutes of washing. You're trapping water that's already there, and that one habit outperforms upgrading to a more expensive cream.

And here's my favorite cheap trick in all of dermatology: at night, on your driest areas — around the nose, the corners of your mouth, your cheeks if you're peeling — put a thin layer of plain petroleum jelly over your moisturizer. Costs almost nothing, works better than most eighty-dollar night creams, and no, it does not clog your pores. I have been saying this for years and I will keep saying it.

**Change 3: Recalibrate Your Actives, Don't Stop Them**

Two opposite mistakes here and people make both.

Mistake one is exfoliating on the same schedule you did all summer. Flaky skin looks like it needs to be scrubbed off. It does not. Flaking in fall is usually a barrier problem, and exfoliating a compromised barrier makes it flake more, so you exfoliate more, and now we're in the loop. Cut your acid or scrub frequency roughly in half when the weather turns, and if your skin is stinging, cut it to zero until it stops stinging.

Mistake two is quitting your retinoid. A lot of people back off retinoids in summer because of sun and irritation, and then never restart. Fall is exactly when you restart. Lower UV, less sweating, and the results have all winter to accumulate.

Restart low and slow even if you were on it before, because your skin loses tolerance. Two nights a week, pea-sized for the whole face, on completely dry skin. If you're irritated, buffer it: moisturizer, then retinoid, then moisturizer. That sandwich reduces irritation without meaningfully hurting the result. Build to nightly over about six weeks. Do not use it the same night as your exfoliating acid while you're rebuilding.

**Change 4: This Is the Pigment Window**

This is the section I actually care about most, and it's the part the "cozy autumn routine" content never covers.

If you have melasma, sun spots, post-inflammatory dark marks from acne, or general uneven tone from the summer — the treatments that fade pigment all have the same weakness, which is that sun undoes them. Trying to fade pigment in July while you're at the lake every weekend is bailing out a boat with a hole in it. Fall through early spring is the window where you can get ahead of it.

Ingredients that actually do something: a retinoid, which we already covered. Azelaic acid, which is underrated and safe in pregnancy. Niacinamide. Vitamin C in the morning. Tranexamic acid topically for melasma. And hydroquinone, which is still the most effective topical we have and which should be used in cycles under supervision, not indefinitely.

And this is also when in-office treatments become appropriate. Chemical peels, IPL for sun spots and redness, and resurfacing lasers all carry more risk when you're actively getting sun, and they all require serious sun protection afterward. So we schedule them in fall and winter. If you've been putting off a treatment for brown spots, this is the eight-week stretch to book it.

**Change 5: Everything That Isn't Your Face**

Your face gets all the attention and it is genuinely the least of your problems in the fall.

Lips: they have essentially no barrier of their own and they crack first. Plain ointment, applied constantly. Skip the ones with menthol, camphor, or "cooling" anything — that tingle is irritation, and it drives a lick-and-reapply cycle that keeps them chapped.

Hands: washing plus cold plus dry air destroys them. Moisturize after every wash, and if they're cracked, do the overnight version — thick ointment and cotton gloves.

Scalp: this is dandruff season. Ketoconazole or zinc pyrithione shampoo, and here's the part people get wrong — it has to sit on your scalp for several minutes to work. Lather, leave it while you do everything else in the shower, then rinse.

And humidity. If your indoor air is bone dry, a humidifier in the bedroom targeting somewhere around forty to fifty percent does more for your skin overnight than most products. Clean it, or you're aerosolizing mold, which is a worse problem than dry skin.

**The Thing Everybody Drops**

Sunscreen. Every year. The temperature drops and it's the first thing to go.

Here's why that's wrong. UVB — the burning ray — does drop off substantially in the fall. But UVA, the one that drives collagen breakdown, wrinkles, and pigment, stays much more constant year-round. It penetrates cloud cover, and it goes through window glass, which means your commute and the desk you sit at next to a window are both dosing you.

Photoaging is not a summer event. It's a slow daily accumulation, and the daily dose is exactly what you're deciding to skip. And if you're doing everything I just described — restarting a retinoid, treating pigment, getting a peel — sunscreen isn't optional, it's the thing that determines whether any of it works.

Then in winter, snow reflects a significant amount of UV back up at you, so if you ski or you're just out in a bright snowy parking lot, you're getting hit from two directions. Ask anyone who's come back from a ski trip with a sunburn under their chin.

**The Fall Task Nobody Does**

Last thing, and it's the most important thing in this video.

You just finished a summer of sun exposure. Now is when you check your skin. Not with a product — with your eyes, in good light, in front of a mirror, including your back with a second mirror or a partner, your scalp, the tops of your ears, the backs of your hands, and your feet including between your toes.

You're looking for anything new, anything changing, anything that bleeds or scabs and won't heal after a few weeks, and any spot that looks different from all your other spots. That last one — the ugly duckling — is one of the most useful concepts we have.

And if you're over forty, or you have a lot of moles, or fair skin, or a family history, or you've had blistering sunburns — get a professional full-body skin check on the calendar. Skin cancer is the most common cancer there is, and caught early it's almost always a minor procedure. Caught late it's a completely different conversation. I have that conversation, and I would like to have it less.
