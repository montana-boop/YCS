**body · 1:00**

**The One Thing That Separates Them**

Let me give you the single most useful piece of information in this entire video, and I'm giving it to you first because I don't want you to miss it.

Go look at your face. Get close to the mirror, good light. You're looking for one thing: comedones. That's the medical word for blackheads and whiteheads. A blackhead is a pore plugged with oil and dead skin where the top is open and the plug has oxidized dark. A whitehead is the same plug with the top closed over.

Here's the rule. Acne makes comedones. Rosacea does not. Rosacea has never made a blackhead in the history of dermatology.

So if you look in the mirror and you see blackheads across your nose, whiteheads on your chin, plugged pores in the T-zone alongside your red bumps — that's acne. If you see red bumps and pus bumps and background redness, but zero blackheads, zero whiteheads, nothing plugged, that is a big flashing sign pointing at rosacea.

That one check gets you most of the way there. Now let me explain what's actually happening in each condition, because once you understand the mechanism, the treatment differences make obvious sense instead of feeling like arbitrary rules.

**What Acne Actually Is**

Acne is a disease of the pore. Four things go wrong, and they go wrong in a specific order.

One: your oil glands make more sebum, usually because of androgens — which is why acne shows up at puberty and why it flares around your cycle. Two: the cells lining the pore stop shedding normally and stick together, forming a plug. That plug is the comedone. Three: a bacteria that lives on everybody's skin, Cutibacterium acnes, thrives in that oxygen-poor plugged environment and multiplies. Four: your immune system shows up angry, and that's the red painful bump.

That order matters, because it tells you where to intervene. If the plug is step two, then the most effective acne treatment on earth is going to be the one that fixes how your pore lining sheds — and that's a retinoid. Everything else is support.

I want you to notice what's not in that list: your face being red all the time, flushing when you drink a glass of wine, your skin burning when you put products on it. Those aren't acne features. Acne is bumps, plugs, and inflammation around the pore. It's not a blood vessel problem.

Rosacea is a blood vessel problem. And that's a completely different animal.

**What Rosacea Actually Is**

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory condition centered on the vessels and nerves of your central face — cheeks, nose, chin, center of the forehead. It affects something like five percent of adults. Millions of people have it, and a huge number of them have no idea, because they've been told their whole lives that they just "have a red face" or "sensitive skin."

Instead of clogged pores, you've got blood vessels that are hyper-reactive and don't know when to calm down, an immune system running hot at the surface, an impaired skin barrier, and often an overgrowth of Demodex, a microscopic mite that lives on everyone's face but shows up in higher numbers in rosacea patients.

Rosacea shows up in a few flavors, and you can have more than one at a time. There's the flushing and persistent redness type, where the background of your face is pink or red and you can see fine broken vessels. There's the bump-and-pustule type, which is the one that gets mistaken for acne every single day. There's the thickening type, where oil glands and tissue on the nose enlarge over years — that's rhinophyma, and no, it's not from drinking, that myth has ruined a lot of people's lives. And there's ocular rosacea, in the eyes: gritty, dry, burning eyes, recurrent styes, red lid margins. That one is wildly underdiagnosed. If you have rosacea on your skin, there's a real chance your eyes are involved too and nobody's connected the dots.

One more thing I need to say, because the textbooks did us dirty for decades. Rosacea is not a fair-skin-only disease. It absolutely occurs in patients with brown and Black skin, where the background redness is harder to see and gets missed for years. If your face feels hot and burns with products and you've got bumps that don't respond to acne treatment, that counts, whatever your skin tone.

**The Seven Questions I Ask**

So here's my actual exam room checklist. Run yourself through it.

One: do you have blackheads or whiteheads? Yes leans acne, no leans rosacea. Two: does your face flush — get hot and red — from heat, exercise, alcohol, spicy food, stress, or walking from a cold parking lot into a warm restaurant? Flushing is rosacea's signature. Acne doesn't flush. Three: does your skin burn or sting when you apply products, even gentle ones? That stinging is rosacea's damaged barrier and irritable nerves talking. Four: where is it? Rosacea parks itself in the center of the face and usually spares the skin right around the eyes. Acne is happy to also live on your jawline, your chest, and your back — rosacea does not go to your back. Five: how old were you when it started? Acne typically starts in the teens. Rosacea typically starts after thirty. Not a hard rule, adult acne is very real, but it's a strong nudge. Six: is there visible fine red vessel branching on your cheeks or the sides of your nose? That's telangiectasia, and it's rosacea. Seven: are your eyes dry, gritty, or getting styes?

I had a patient last year, mid-forties, who'd been on three different acne regimens from three different providers. She came in frustrated and honestly a little embarrassed. She had zero comedones. Zero. She flushed at the mention of red wine, her cheeks had visible vessels, and she'd been scrubbing with a benzoyl peroxide wash twice a day because that's what you do for acne. We stopped everything, put her on the right treatment, and eight weeks later she looked like a different person. She'd spent two years fighting the wrong disease.

**Why the Wrong Treatment Makes It Worse**

This is the part I really want you to internalize, because this is where the actual damage happens.

A standard acne routine is built to strip and dry. Benzoyl peroxide, salicylic acid, an astringent toner, a foaming sulfate cleanser, physical exfoliation, a strong retinoid. For a person with oily, comedonal acne, that combination can be appropriate.

Now put that same routine on rosacea skin, which already has an impaired barrier and vessels that overreact to everything. You strip the barrier further, water evaporates out faster, the nerve endings get more exposed and more irritable, and the vessels dilate harder in response to the irritation. So the face gets redder. And what does a person do when their face gets redder and bumpier? They assume it's worse acne and they go harder. Stronger product, more frequent, add another active.

That's the loop. I see people who've been in it for years, and by the time they reach me their barrier is so wrecked that we have to spend the first month doing almost nothing but repairing it before we can even start treating the rosacea.

So if there's one thing you take from this video: if your "acne" gets angrier every time you treat it more aggressively, that is a diagnostic clue. Real acne responds to acne treatment. It might take twelve weeks, but it moves in the right direction. If it moves the wrong direction, you're treating the wrong disease.

**What Actually Works for Acne**

Let's do treatment, starting with acne, and I'm going to keep this practical.

The foundation is a retinoid, because that's the ingredient that fixes the plug. Over the counter you can get adapalene 0.1% — Differin — for about fifteen bucks, and it used to be prescription-only. Start three nights a week on dry skin, pea-sized amount for the whole face, and build up. Expect it to get slightly worse before it gets better around weeks three to six. That's purging, that's the plugs coming to the surface, and it's the number one reason people quit right before it works.

Add benzoyl peroxide to hit the bacteria, 2.5 to 5 percent — higher isn't better, it's just more irritating. It bleaches towels and pillowcases, so use white ones or you'll be mad at me. If you're dealing with hormonal acne along the jawline that flares with your cycle, that's a conversation about spironolactone or a combined oral contraceptive with your doctor, and honestly, that conversation changes lives. And if you have deep painful nodules that are leaving scars, do not spend two years on drugstore products. Scarring acne is a reason to get in with a dermatologist now, because we can't undo scars nearly as well as we can prevent them.

Support that with a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer that doesn't clog — yes, oily skin needs moisturizer — and sunscreen.

**What Actually Works for Rosacea**

Rosacea is a different toolbox entirely, and the first tool isn't a medication.

Step one is subtraction. Gentle cleanser, no foaming sulfates, lukewarm water, no scrubs, no washcloths, no brushes. A bland barrier-repair moisturizer with ceramides. And sunscreen, every day — UV is the single most reliable rosacea trigger there is, and mineral sunscreens with zinc tend to be better tolerated on reactive skin. For a lot of patients, subtraction alone drops their redness noticeably in a few weeks.

Then we add targeted prescriptions. Azelaic acid works on the bumps and the redness and is well tolerated. Ivermectin cream targets the Demodex piece and can be excellent for the papules and pustules. Metronidazole is the old reliable. For the persistent background redness there are prescription creams that constrict the vessels — those are cosmetic and temporary, and one of them can cause rebound redness, so that's a discussion, not a default. For moderate to severe cases we use low-dose doxycycline at an anti-inflammatory dose, which is deliberately below the level that acts as an antibiotic, so you get the inflammation control without the resistance concerns.

And for the broken vessels and the fixed background redness, no cream on earth is going to erase those. That's a light and laser conversation — IPL or a pulsed dye laser. Those actually target the vessel itself, and the results can be dramatic. Fall and winter are the right time for that, once your summer sun exposure is behind you.

Trigger management matters too. Not to live in fear of everything, but to know your own list. Keep a two-week note on your phone of what you were doing when you flared. Most people find three or four repeat offenders and can work around them.

**What If You Have Both?**

Because plenty of you are sitting there thinking "I have blackheads AND I flush," and you're not wrong.

Acne and rosacea can absolutely coexist, especially in adults in their thirties and forties. When that happens, we treat both, but we lead with the rosacea, because rosacea skin can't tolerate a full-strength acne assault. That means gentle everything, azelaic acid does double duty here since it helps both, a very slow low-strength retinoid introduction if the skin will take it, and we skip the harsh stuff entirely.

There are also two impostors worth naming. Perioral dermatitis is red bumps clustered around the mouth and nose, classically sparing a thin rim right at the lip line, and it's frequently caused by topical steroids or heavy occlusive products. Steroid creams make it feel better for a few days and then dramatically worse, and people chase that cycle for months. And there's fungal folliculitis — uniform itchy little bumps, often on the forehead, chest and back, that never respond to any acne treatment because it's yeast, not bacteria.

If you've been treating your face for over three months with no meaningful progress, that's your signal. Not a stronger product. A diagnosis.
